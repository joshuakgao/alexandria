# Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms

![[2017-ppo-thumbnail.png]]

## Research gap

Policy gradient methods for RL face a trade-off: vanilla policy gradients are simple but have poor data efficiency and robustness (one gradient update per data sample, destructively large updates with multiple epochs); TRPO achieves reliable monotonic improvement via constrained optimization but is complex to implement, requires second-order optimization (conjugate gradient), and is incompatible with parameter sharing, dropout, and auxiliary tasks. No first-order method achieves TRPO's reliability while remaining simple and general.

## Contributions

- Introduces **Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)**, a family of policy gradient methods that enable multiple epochs of minibatch updates on the same trajectory data via a clipped surrogate objective.
- Proposes the **clipped surrogate objective** (CLIP), which clips the probability ratio r_t(θ) to [1-ε, 1+ε], forming a pessimistic lower bound on the unclipped objective that prevents destructively large policy updates without second-order optimization.
- Demonstrates that PPO outperforms TRPO, A2C, ACER, CEM, and vanilla policy gradients across continuous control (MuJoCo) and discrete control (49 Atari games) benchmarks.
- Provides an alternative adaptive KL penalty variant, though the clipped version performs better empirically.

## Method

PPO alternates between collecting trajectory data and optimizing a surrogate objective:

1. **Data collection**: N parallel actors each run the current policy for T timesteps, collecting transitions and computing advantage estimates via Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE, λ=0.95).

2. **Clipped surrogate objective**: For K epochs, optimize over minibatches:
   - L^CLIP(θ) = E_t[min(r_t(θ) · A_t, clip(r_t(θ), 1-ε, 1+ε) · A_t)]
   - where r_t(θ) = π_θ(a_t|s_t) / π_θ_old(a_t|s_t) is the probability ratio
   - The clip removes incentive for moving r_t outside [1-ε, 1+ε], and the min ensures the objective is a lower bound — changes that would improve the objective are ignored if they require too large a policy shift.

3. **Combined loss**: When sharing parameters between policy and value function:
   - L = L^CLIP - c1 · L^VF + c2 · S[π_θ]
   - where L^VF is squared value function error and S is an entropy bonus for exploration.

Key hyperparameters: ε=0.2 (continuous control) or ε=0.1 linearly annealed (Atari), K=10 epochs (continuous) or K=3 (Atari), Adam optimizer.

**Adaptive KL penalty variant**: Instead of clipping, penalize KL divergence with an adaptive coefficient β that doubles when KL exceeds 1.5× target and halves when below target/1.5. Simpler but performs slightly worse than clipping.

## Datasets & evaluation

**Continuous control (MuJoCo via OpenAI Gym):**
- 7 tasks: HalfCheetah, Hopper, InvertedDoublePendulum, InvertedPendulum, Reacher, Swimmer, Walker2d.
- PPO (clip ε=0.2) achieves best normalized score (0.82) vs. adaptive KL (0.74), fixed KL (best 0.72), and no clipping/penalty (-0.39).
- Outperforms TRPO, A2C, A2C+Trust Region, CEM, and vanilla PG on almost all tasks.

**3D humanoid (Roboschool):**
- Locomotion, flag-run, and flag-run-harder (with obstacle cubes). Demonstrates scaling to high-dimensional continuous control (17 joints).

**Atari (49 games, ALE):**
- PPO wins 30/49 games on average training reward vs. A2C (1 game) and ACER (18 games).
- On final performance (last 100 episodes): PPO wins 19, ACER 28, A2C 1 — PPO matches ACER's final performance while being much simpler.
- Same CNN architecture as A3C (Mnih et al., 2016).

## Limitations

- The clipped objective is a heuristic — no theoretical guarantee of monotonic improvement (unlike TRPO's constrained optimization), though it works well empirically.
- Hyperparameter ε requires tuning per domain (0.2 for continuous, 0.1 with annealing for Atari).
- On-policy: requires fresh trajectory data each iteration, making it less sample-efficient than off-policy methods (DQN, SAC) for some problems.
- Performance on some Atari games (e.g., Montezuma's Revenge: score 42) remains far below human level, reflecting the broader sparse-reward exploration challenge.
- No comparison to off-policy methods (DQN, DDPG) which may be more sample-efficient in certain settings.

## Key takeaways

- The clipped surrogate objective is a remarkably simple mechanism — essentially just a min and a clip — that achieves the stability benefits of trust region methods without second-order optimization, making PPO implementable in "a few lines of code change" from vanilla policy gradients.
- PPO became the de facto standard on-policy RL algorithm due to this simplicity-performance trade-off: it is the default algorithm in most RL libraries and was used to train ChatGPT (RLHF), making it arguably the most widely deployed RL algorithm in practice.
- The key insight is that pessimistic bounds (taking the minimum of clipped and unclipped objectives) prevent catastrophic policy updates while still allowing steady improvement — a design principle that extends beyond RL to any iterative optimization where stability matters.
- Multiple epochs of minibatch updates on the same trajectory data dramatically improve sample efficiency over vanilla policy gradients (which use each sample once), bridging the gap between on-policy simplicity and off-policy efficiency.
